equalizer Why 1/24-Octave RTA Resolution Matters at FOH

In live sound reinforcement, standard 31-band (1/3-octave) graphic spectrum displays average acoustic energy across wide frequency windows. This broad averaging hides narrow room resonance modes and makes it difficult to pinpoint the exact frequency causing a microphone to squeal.

Surgical Feedback Isolation & Room Mode Detection

A 1/24th-octave analyzer divides the spectrum into 240 logarithmic bands with a sharp filter quality factor (Q ≈ 34.7). This matches the bandwidth of parametric EQ notch filters on modern digital mixing consoles, allowing system engineers to notch out ringing feedback frequencies with surgical precision without thinning out vocal warmth or instrument body.

Whether ringing out stage monitor wedges before rehearsal or checking house curve response with pink noise, high-resolution fractional octave analysis shows you the acoustic reality of the room rather than a smeared approximation.

help Frequently Asked Questions

Why is 1/24-octave RTA resolution superior to standard 1/3-octave for live sound?
A standard 1/3-octave RTA divides the audio spectrum into only 31 broad bands, smearing narrow acoustic room modes and micro-feedback rings. A 1/24-octave RTA delivers 240 distinct logarithmic bands with a filter Q of ~34.7, matching modern digital console parametric EQ filters for surgical feedback notching without hollowing out vocal warmth.
How do Fast vs. Slow ballistics help during live soundcheck?
Fast ballistics (125 ms decay) reveal transient acoustic spikes, vocal plosives, and instantaneous feedback frequencies in real time. Slow ballistics (1,000 ms decay) average out room reflections and pink noise fluctuations, providing a stable visual representation of the venue's overall tonal balance for PA house curve tuning.
Can I connect an external measurement microphone for live RTA calibration?
Yes. While built-in smartphone microphones are suitable for quick checks between 100 Hz and 8 kHz, SoundEngg Pro supports external USB-C measurement microphones (such as miniDSP UMIK, Dayton Audio iMM-6, or Earthworks) and imports individual calibration files (.cal / .txt) to achieve flat reference measurement down to 20 Hz.
